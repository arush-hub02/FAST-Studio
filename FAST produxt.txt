2. Product vision

2.1 What is FAST?

FAST stands for Free Ad-Supported Streaming Television.

A FAST channel behaves like a traditional television channel delivered over the internet. Programming follows a continuous schedule, and viewers can tune in at any time without selecting every individual movie or episode.

The channel operator typically earns revenue through advertising, distribution agreements or other commercial arrangements.

A FAST platform provides the operational tools needed to create, manage, schedule, distribute and monitor these channels.

2.2 Product vision

FAST Studio is a proposed cloud-based platform that allows media companies to transform existing video libraries into scheduled, continuously playing television channels.

The platform should make the process understandable and manageable through a unified administrative interface.

A user should be able to:

1. Sign in securely.
2. Create or access an organization.
3. Import and manage video content.
4. Define content metadata and licensing rights.
5. Create a television channel.
6. Schedule content into a continuous programme lineup.
7. Configure advertising opportunities.
8. Preview the channel and programme guide.
9. Simulate publishing and distribution.
10. Monitor channel health and view operational analytics.

The prototype should demonstrate this complete workflow with a consistent domain model and persistent data.

2.3 Product positioning

FAST Studio is a working concept for a platform in the same general category as SoFast and Evrideo.

It is not intended to be an exact copy of either product.

The prototype should explore a combination of:

• Media asset management.
• FAST channel creation.
• Programme scheduling.
• Electronic Programme Guide (EPG) generation.
• Content-rights management.
• Geographic restrictions.
• Advertising configuration.
• Channel preview and playout simulation.
• Distribution management.
• Operational monitoring.
• Analytics and reporting.
• Organization and user administration.

2.4 Primary objectives

Business objectives

• Demonstrate a credible FAST product concept to company leadership.
• Establish a shared understanding of the product's scope.
• Identify a commercially useful MVP.
• Show how content owners and channel operators could use the platform.
• Identify capabilities that can be built internally versus integrated with third-party services.

Technical objectives

• Establish one coherent React or Angular frontend and a Java/Spring Boot backend architecture.
• Define the core domain model and API boundaries.
• Demonstrate end-to-end workflows using realistic data.
• Create interfaces for future media-processing, playout, ad and distribution integrations.
• Make it possible to replace mock services incrementally without redesigning the entire UI.

2.5 Out of scope for the initial prototype

Unless explicitly approved, the initial prototype will not claim to provide:

• Production-grade 24/7 broadcast reliability.
• Commercial-scale video transcoding.
• Guaranteed global CDN delivery.
• Real advertising revenue.
• Actual third-party platform distribution.
• Production DRM or entitlement security.
• Real audience measurement without a genuine data source.
• Guaranteed legal compliance across every country.
• A fully automated broadcast control room.

These may become later engineering projects.

3. Users, personas and access control

The product must distinguish the person operating the platform from the people managing channels and content.

3.1 Platform Super Administrator

This role manages the platform itself.

Responsibilities:

• Create, suspend and manage customer organizations.
• Manage platform-level configuration.
• Inspect system-wide health.
• View integration status.
• Manage platform-level feature availability.
• Review security and audit events.
• Manage system-wide settings.

This role should not automatically be available to ordinary organization administrators.

3.2 Organization Owner

The customer organization owner manages its own account and operations.

Responsibilities:

• Configure organization details.
• Invite users.
• Assign organization roles.
• Manage channels and content.
• Configure rights and distribution settings.
• Review analytics and operational status.
• Configure organization-specific integrations.

3.3 Organization Administrator

Responsibilities:

• Manage content, channels and schedules.
• Configure applicable rights.
• Manage users within permitted limits.
• Review operations and reports.
• Manage organization settings when authorized.

The Organization Owner retains ownership-level permissions.

3.4 Content Manager

Responsibilities:

• Import assets.
• Edit metadata.
• Manage artwork, subtitles and language information.
• Review media processing status.
• Manage content availability.
• View asset licensing information.
• Flag content requiring rights or quality review.

3.5 Channel Operator

Responsibilities:

• Create or edit channels, subject to permissions.
• Build playlists.
• Manage schedules.
• Preview programmes.
• Review playout status.
• Resolve operational issues.
• Publish or activate channels if permitted.

3.6 Ad Operations Manager

Responsibilities:

• Configure ad campaigns.
• Create ad-break definitions.
• Manage campaign eligibility.
• View simulated or actual ad-delivery reports.
• Review advertising configuration.

3.7 Analyst / Read-only User

Responsibilities:

• View dashboards and reports.
• Inspect channels, schedules and performance.
• Export permitted reports.
• Avoid making operational changes.

3.8 Role-based access control

Implement role-based access control (RBAC) with permissions enforced by the backend in any API-backed implementation.

Suggested permission names:

• "organizations.read"
• "organizations.manage"
• "users.read"
• "users.invite"
• "users.manage"
• "assets.read"
• "assets.create"
• "assets.update"
• "assets.delete"
• "rights.read"
• "rights.manage"
• "channels.read"
• "channels.create"
• "channels.update"
• "channels.publish"
• "schedules.read"
• "schedules.manage"
• "ads.read"
• "ads.manage"
• "analytics.read"

Use least-privilege access. Do not rely on hiding navigation links as a security control.

For the prototype, demonstrate at least Super Administrator, Organization Administrator, Content Manager, Channel Operator and Analyst views.

5. Authentication and onboarding

Authentication is the first functional module.

5.1 Required screens

A. Sign-in page

Fields:

• Email address.
• Password.
• Show/hide password.
• Remember me, if supported by the chosen authentication mechanism.
• Sign-in button.
• Forgot password link.
• Link to sign-up, if self-registration is enabled.

Requirements:

• Validate required fields.
• Validate email format.
• Show loading state while submitting.
• Display safe, useful error messages.
• Prevent repeated submissions while a request is in progress.
• Redirect authenticated users to the correct landing page.
• Avoid revealing whether an account exists in a password-related error message.

B. Registration / organization creation

For the prototype, support a configurable onboarding workflow.

Possible fields:

• Full name.
• Work email.
• Password.
• Organization name.
• Organization type.
• Country.
• Preferred time zone.
• Acceptance of terms, if required by the demonstration flow.

Successful registration should create or associate the user with an organization and assign the appropriate initial role.

For production, account verification and invitation policies must be defined before launch.

C. Forgot password

• Accept an email address.
• Display a generic confirmation.
• Integrate with a real email service only when configured.
• Use a development-only simulated reset flow if no email provider is available.
• Never expose password-reset tokens in ordinary production logs.

D. Reset password

• Validate the reset token.
• Validate password requirements.
• Confirm password changes.
• Invalidate the token after use.
• Display expired or invalid token states.

E. Email verification

Optional for the initial demonstration, but include the workflow in the architecture.

F. Invitation acceptance

• Validate the invitation.
• Associate the user with the correct organization.
• Assign the role specified by the authorized inviter.
• Require the user to establish credentials or authenticate.
• Handle expired and revoked invitations.

G. Access denied and session expiry

• Provide a clear access-denied page.
• Handle expired sessions.
• Redirect to sign-in when necessary.
• Preserve the intended destination when safe to do so.

5.2 Authentication architecture

If a backend is included, evaluate a mature identity provider or a well-maintained authentication library before implementing authentication from scratch.

The architecture must support:

• Secure password handling if passwords are managed locally.
• Session or token lifecycle management.
• Logout and session invalidation.
• Backend authentication middleware.
• Role and permission checks.
• Rate limiting on sensitive endpoints.
• Secure password reset.
• CSRF protection where applicable.
• Appropriate CORS configuration.
• Secure cookie handling when cookie-based sessions are used.

Do not store sensitive authentication tokens in browser local storage by default. Choose and document an appropriate session model.

5.3 Demo authentication

Provide a clearly labelled development/demo authentication mode when necessary.

Requirements:

• Seed documented demo accounts for different roles.
• Make it possible to switch between roles during a demonstration.
• Do not present demo credentials as production credentials.
• Prevent demo-mode shortcuts from being enabled silently in production builds.
• Ensure all demo users and their data are fictional.

10. Playlist and scheduling engine

Scheduling is one of the most important modules.

The prototype must demonstrate a meaningful programme scheduling workflow rather than a simple list of video titles.

10.1 Schedule concepts

A schedule defines which programme should play during a particular time interval.

Each scheduled item should reference:

• Channel.
• Media asset.
• Start time.
• End time.
• Duration.
• Schedule status.
• Optional advertising breaks.
• Optional recurrence configuration.

Store timestamps in a consistent format, preferably UTC, and present them in the appropriate display time zone.

10.2 Scheduler views

Provide:

• Day view.
• Timeline or grid view.
• List view.
• Current and upcoming programmes.
• Date navigation.
• Channel filter.
• Schedule warnings.
• Programme detail panel.

For a multi-channel view, each channel may occupy a separate horizontal lane.

10.3 Add programme

The operator should be able to:

1. Select a channel.
2. Select an eligible asset.
3. Choose a start time.
4. Set a duration or end time.
5. Review rights and asset readiness.
6. Save the programme.
7. See the updated timeline.

Drag-and-drop scheduling may be implemented if it improves usability, but must not compromise basic accessibility or reliable editing.

10.4 Schedule validation

The system should detect:

• Overlapping programmes.
• Missing or unavailable assets.
• Negative or zero durations.
• Invalid start/end times.
• Content rights that expire before playback completes.
• Geographic restrictions that conflict with channel availability.
• Gaps in the schedule.
• Duplicate or conflicting schedule entries.
• Invalid recurring schedules.
• Daylight-saving-time ambiguities where applicable.

Validation should distinguish between:

• Blocking errors.
• Warnings.
• Informational notices.

10.5 Continuous channel behavior

A FAST channel generally needs continuous programming.

Support at least one scheduling strategy in the prototype:

• Fixed schedule.
• Playlist loop.
• Repeat until the next scheduled item.
• Fallback content when an eligible item is missing.

Document the selected strategy.

Do not automatically fill every gap without showing the operator how and why the gap was handled.

10.6 Schedule publishing

Support a draft/published distinction.

Suggested workflow:

1. Edit the draft schedule.
2. Validate it.
3. Review warnings.
4. Publish the schedule version.
5. Display the published version and effective time.
6. Keep the previous version or an audit record for rollback.

The prototype may simulate publishing, but it must retain meaningful version history.

10.7 Schedule versioning

Record:

• Version identifier.
• Channel identifier.
• Created by.
• Creation time.
• Publication time.
• Validation result.
• Changes from the previous version.

This helps demonstrate how a broadcast operation can be audited.

12. Rights management and geo-blocking

Rights management should be a first-class product module rather than an optional setting buried inside a channel form.

12.1 Rights concepts

Distinguish between:

• Asset rights.
• Channel rights.
• Distribution rights.
• Platform-specific restrictions.
• Advertising eligibility, where applicable.

A rights policy may include:

• Policy name.
• Organization.
• Associated assets or channels.
• Allowed countries.
• Blocked countries.
• Rights start date.
• Rights end date.
• Permitted distribution platforms.
• Available languages or versions.
• Optional DRM requirement.
• Policy status.
• Audit history.

12.2 Geographic rules

For the prototype, implement a clearly labelled playback-access simulator.

Example:

• A movie is licensed for India and the United Kingdom.
• The movie is unavailable in the United States and Canada.
• The operator requests a playback decision for each country.
• The UI displays the decision, the reason and the applicable policy.

The test must evaluate the effective rules, not just the channel-level allowlist.

12.3 Effective rights policy

Define a documented precedence model.

Recommended starting principle:

• Explicit prohibitions cannot be overridden by a more permissive rule at another level.
• The content must be eligible for the requested territory, time window, platform and distribution context.
• Missing or contradictory rights information should produce a visible validation issue.
• The platform must not silently infer permission from the absence of a restriction.

Codex should propose the exact precedence algorithm and test it before implementation.

12.4 Rights windows

Support:

• Scheduled activation.
• Expiry.
• Time-zone-safe display.
• Warnings for upcoming expiry.
• Blocking playback when rights are not currently valid.
• Schedule validation for programmes that extend beyond the permitted rights window.

Define boundary behavior explicitly: for example, whether a programme may begin before expiry and finish after expiry. The default should be to reject a schedule that violates the configured rights policy unless an authorized exception is explicitly supported.

12.5 Production geo-blocking

A production implementation requires server-side enforcement.

Potential components:

• Trusted IP-geolocation service.
• Backend rights evaluator.
• Playback authorization endpoint.
• Short-lived playback token or signed URL.
• Stream or CDN authorization rules.
• Cache behavior that respects access policies.
• Logging of relevant authorization decisions.

Do not treat a user-selected country, browser locale or frontend dropdown as trustworthy evidence of location.

VPNs and geolocation errors must be considered.

12.6 DRM

DRM is distinct from geo-blocking.

Geo-blocking restricts access by location. DRM protects encrypted media and controls access through supported playback licences.

Include DRM configuration as an architectural placeholder unless a real DRM provider is selected and configured.

13. Advertising and monetization

The initial prototype should demonstrate the workflows while making clear which data is simulated.

• Active campaigns.
• Scheduled campaigns.

13.2 Campaign management

Fields:

• Campaign name.
• Campaign identifier.
• Advertiser.
• Start and end dates.
• Campaign status.
• Associated channels.
• Target territories.
• Target audience rules, if included.
• Budget or impression target, if required.
• Priority.
• External ad-provider reference, if configured.

Support create, edit, pause and archive operations according to permissions.

13.3 Ad-break management

Allow the operator to configure advertising opportunities within the channel schedule.

Fields:

• Channel.
• Associated programme or schedule entry.
• Break position.
• Planned duration.
• Eligible campaign.
• Break status.

Ad breaks may be pre-roll, mid-roll or post-roll where appropriate.

13.4 Advertising terminology

The documentation and UI should distinguish:

• Ad break: the planned opportunity to show advertisements.
• Ad decision: selection of eligible advertisements.
• Ad insertion: placing ads into the playback experience.
• Impression: an event defined by the applicable measurement standard or provider.
• Fill rate: the proportion of eligible ad opportunities filled under a defined calculation.
• Revenue: actual or estimated commercial value based on a specified data source.

13.5 Ad insertion architecture

The system should be designed to support a future ad decisioning or SSAI provider.

SCTE-35 signals may be relevant to supported streaming workflows.

The prototype can represent ad markers on a timeline and simulate ad selection.

It must not claim to perform real server-side ad insertion without a working media integration.

13.6 Revenue calculations

If simulated revenue is displayed, document the formula and assumptions.

For example, an illustrative revenue estimate may be calculated from simulated impressions and an assumed CPM.

Never mix simulated and real data without clear labels and a known data source.

13.7 Platform subscriptions and billable usage

FAST Studio earns B2B platform revenue separately from publisher advertising. Start with a base subscription, channel allowance and disclosed usage for storage, processing, delivery or SSAI only where contracted. Add optional setup, managed operations, localization and integration services. Version prices/entitlements and enforce them in Java.

Include trial conversion, hosted checkout or invoicing, authoritative payment confirmation, allowances/overage alerts, credits, cancellation, renewal changes and payment-failure grace. Persist source-backed usage events and deduplicate retries. Do not stop existing channels automatically because of a delayed billing callback.

13.8 Advertising sales and settlement

Add advertisers, insertion orders, line items, approved creatives, inventory ownership/reservations, pacing, pod limits and direct-sales delivery obligations. Integrate one ad server/SSAI provider and one demand path before adding yield optimization. Support sponsorships and approved QR deliverables only when the media path and contract allow them.

Import authoritative measured reports, identify discrepancies/invalid-traffic adjustments and apply effective-dated revenue contracts. Keep inventory splits (opportunities) separate from revenue splits (money). Finance approves statements and adjustments independently; preserve source and contract version evidence. Automated publisher payouts are deferred beyond statement/export and payment reconciliation.

13.9 Revenue quality and channel economics

Clearly distinguish simulated, observed, estimated and reconciled metrics; cash receipt is a separate state. Ad decisions are not billable impressions. Show reporting lag, currency, source, metric denominator and coverage. Never sum simulated and real data. Show N/A or incomplete when audience/cost inputs are missing.

Track net ad revenue per watch hour, publisher contribution, platform MRR/churn, attributable costs and unpaid receivables. Channel profitability includes rights, delivery, operations and platform charges without double-counting provider costs. Use exact decimal calculations and deterministic rounding in Java.

See docs/MONETIZATION_STRATEGY.md for packaging, meter definitions, contract/settlement workflow and an internally consistent illustrative revenue and break-even calculation. Its example rates are assumptions, not market prices or promised returns.

22. Technical architecture

The architecture must be refined after inspecting the repository and confirming the team's backend capabilities.

22.1 Frontend

Use React or Angular with TypeScript, paired with a Java/Spring Boot backend. Angular is the provisional default because it matches the original intended developer. Select one frontend based on actual team capability; do not build two parallel administration applications.

Suggested areas:

• Core application shell.
• Authentication and session management.
• Shared UI components.
• Organization management.
• Media library.
• Channel management.
• Scheduling.
• EPG.
• Rights management.
• Advertising.
• Distribution.
• Monitoring.
• Analytics.
• Settings.

Use feature-based organization rather than putting all components, API calls and business rules into one module.

Preserve established React/Angular conventions when code is present. No frontend or backend code exists in the inspected workspace; select and pin supported versions during kickoff.

22.2 Frontend principles

• Strict typing.
• Reusable components.
• Reactive forms or the project's established form approach.
• Route guards for navigation.
• HTTP interceptors or equivalent centralized request handling.
• Centralized error handling.
• Accessible controls.
• Loading, empty, success and error states.
• Clear separation of API models and presentation models.
• No hardcoded business rules scattered across components.
• Avoid oversized components that mix unrelated responsibilities.

Use supported patterns for the selected framework: typed reactive forms in Angular, or typed forms and the selected routing/server-state conventions in React. Keep authoritative scheduling, rights, quota and financial rules in Java.

22.3 Backend

The backend requirement is Java. Use a Spring Boot modular monolith with Spring MVC, Spring Security, Bean Validation, PostgreSQL, Spring Data JPA and Flyway migrations. Object storage holds media; durable jobs/outbox processing handle provider operations and report imports.

Java 21 is a provisional baseline; evaluate Java 25 and the supported Spring Boot line against existing services and SDKs before pinning. Reuse existing Enveu Java services where their actual capabilities fit. Node.js/NestJS/Express are not the backend proposal for this revision.

Use secure OIDC sessions, server-enforced tenant permissions, optimistic concurrency for schedules, exact decimal financial calculations and idempotent usage/payment/report ingestion. Media streaming, transcoding and SSAI run through provider adapters rather than through the Java request path.

Detailed decisions, consistency rules, performance envelopes and recovery targets are in docs/TECHNICAL_ARCHITECTURE.md. They are proposed engineering requirements, not verified production capabilities.

22.4 Suggested backend domains

• Identity and authorization.
• Organizations and memberships.
• Media assets.
• Channel configuration.
• Scheduling.
• Rights management.
• Advertising.
• Distribution.
• Monitoring and alerts.
• Analytics.
• Audit and notifications.

Start with a modular monolith unless there is a concrete reason for separate deployable services.

Do not introduce microservices, Kubernetes, Kafka or other infrastructure solely to make the prototype appear sophisticated.

• Rights evaluation rejects unauthorized requests.

28. Non-functional requirements

Performance

• Avoid unnecessary full-page reloads.
• Use pagination or appropriate virtualization for large lists.
• Avoid loading all media metadata when a smaller page is sufficient.
• Keep expensive processing outside synchronous request handlers.
• Profile the actual implementation rather than making unsupported performance claims.

Reliability

• Display explicit errors when operations fail.
• Avoid silently losing form changes.
• Preserve published schedule history.
• Handle missing media and unavailable integrations.
• Make retries safe where applicable.

Security

• Use least-privilege authorization.
• Enforce tenant boundaries.
• Validate all API input.
• Protect authentication flows.
• Avoid exposing secrets.
• Record significant administrative actions.
• Apply secure file-upload validation.
• Define appropriate retention and deletion behavior.

Accessibility

• Use semantic controls.
• Support keyboard navigation.
• Provide labels and error descriptions.
• Maintain sufficient contrast.
• Do not communicate status using color alone.
• Ensure the scheduler has a usable non-drag-and-drop interaction.

Maintainability

• Separate business logic from UI components.
• Use typed domain models.
• Keep external integrations behind adapters.
• Provide migrations and reproducible seed data when a backend is used.
• Document environment setup.
• Keep test and lint commands working.

29. Implementation roadmap

Planning baseline: 19 October 2026. Status: all releases unstarted; discovery inspection and this plan are complete, but R0 customer/provider validation remains open.

## Delivery assumptions

Use one React or Angular TypeScript application and a Java/Spring Boot modular monolith. Angular is the reversible default from the original brief; the plan supports React with the same API and domain rules. Start with PostgreSQL, object storage and durable background jobs. Integrate media delivery and ad insertion through providers.

Indicative elapsed ranges assume two frontend engineers, two Java engineers, one QA engineer, a shared product/UX lead, and a media/ad-tech specialist available during R2–R3. Work can overlap within a release. Ranges include integration and hardening work, but exclude unpredictable partner procurement/certification delays. A smaller team must re-estimate. These are planning estimates, not delivery commitments.

| Release | Indicative duration / cumulative time | Main outcome | Feature scope | Accountable lead |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| R0: validate and design | 1–2 weeks / 1–2 | Confirm pilot buyer, framework, provider path and commercial hypothesis | Discovery for F01–F25 | Product lead + technical lead |
| R1: leadership prototype | 6–8 weeks / 7–10 | Demonstrate content → rights → schedule → EPG → preview → ad/distribution simulation | F01–F10 | Engineering lead |

| R4: expansion tranche | 8–12 weeks per selected tranche | Expand proven revenue paths and customer segments | Select F27–F38 by demand; not all in one tranche | Product + commercial lead |

## R0 — confirm the smallest sellable outcome

1. Interview five content owners/operators and identify two prospective pilot customers. Record catalog size, territories, operating effort, current costs, ad inventory ownership and purchase authority.
2. Choose React or Angular from actual team capability; use Angular provisionally. Confirm Java LTS/runtime, Spring Boot support and provider SDK compatibility before pinning versions.
3. Inventory reusable Enveu identity, storage, media processing, player, analytics and billing services. No such code is present in this workspace.
4. Obtain technical access and commercial requirements for one playout/SSAI path and one destination. Prepare a configurable simulator if access is delayed.
5. Define pilot coverage, broadcaster responsibilities, billing currency, billable meters, inventory split, measurement authority and settlement ownership.
6. Produce the API contract, rights rules, seed scenarios and first-slice acceptance cases from this document pack.

**Exit evidence:** named pilot owners, selected frontend, approved Java approach, provider feasibility checklist, agreed pilot scope and a reviewed backlog. Customer/provider access is a gate to paid rollout, not a reason to stop local prototype work.

## R1 — working product narrative

**Exit evidence:** repeatable clean-start demo, persistent data, complete keyboard workflow, permission checks, critical business tests, setup instructions and implemented/simulated/unavailable feature matrix. No claims of a live continuous broadcast or collected ad revenue.

## R2 — first paying platform customer

- F11: versioned plans and contracts, trial/active/past-due/canceled subscriptions, backend feature and quota enforcement.
- F12: authoritative tenant usage events, replay-safe aggregation, allowances and overage estimates. Alert before limits; prevent new paid resources without unexpectedly stopping existing broadcasts.
- F13: one hosted billing provider or invoice adapter, real settlement confirmation, signed webhook deduplication, credits, cancellations and payment-failure recovery. Test mode and live mode remain separate.
- F14–F15: resumable upload, bulk import preview, media QC evidence and real processing results for pilot assets.
- F16: approval separation, optimistic concurrency, activity history and production-safe identities/secrets.
- Operational baseline: deployment rollback, restore test, monitoring, support owner, tenant exports and documented retention policy.

**Dependencies:** billing-provider availability, legal entity/currency choices, commercial agreement, real media processing access and R1 workflow correctness.

**Exit evidence:** pilot user loads a catalog; signed-in billing owner sees the same usage as the invoice; payment/invoice settlement is reconciled; replay does not double-bill; downgrade and delinquency policies pass; no cross-tenant access. A sandbox checkout alone does not satisfy the commercial gate. If payment rails are unavailable, use an agreed invoice workflow with independently verified payment and clearly record the adapter limitation.

## R3 — earn and explain actual advertising revenue

| Workstream | Requirements | Launch gate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stream and partner delivery | F17; playout adapter, eligible rendition, published EPG, signed authorization, one destination | Agreed 72-hour initial soak, stream continuity checks, rollback and rights revocation drill; partner approval recorded |
| Ads and direct sales | F18–F20; orders/line items, duration reservations, inventory ownership, pacing, pods, creative approval, frequency limits | Provider test matrix passes including malformed tags, timeout, missing markers, underfill and slate; actual approved test insertion |

Start the 72-hour soak only after representative programme transitions and ad breaks are configured. Extend it when defects or insufficient coverage invalidate the evidence. This soak is an initial pilot gate, not proof of an annual availability SLA.

**Commercial gate:** two pilots review actual subscription and channel economics and renew or commit to continue. Prove demand and manageable operating cost before broad destination or ad-provider expansion. Real ad cash settlement may lag the technical pilot by the partner's payment cycle; record outstanding receivables rather than calling them cash received.

## R4 — select expansion by evidence

1. **Yield and regional growth:** F27–F28 after sufficient comparable impressions, a second demand contract or a signed new-territory customer. Compare net revenue per watch hour alongside retention and errors.
2. **Enterprise and partner sales:** F29–F32 when a qualified contract requires SSO/SCIM, APIs, reseller access or managed services. Price incremental support and infrastructure.
3. **Format expansion:** F33–F34 after live/catch-up rights, media capability and a paying use case are verified.
4. **AI and commerce experiments:** F35–F37 after approved source content/catalog, human review, cost limits and a measured experiment goal. Build manual sponsorships before automated product detection.
5. **Premium resilience:** F38 after measured failover/restore behavior and a costed service commitment.

Each selected tranche gets its own capacity estimate, acceptance criteria and commercial success test. Do not bundle all expansion ideas into a promised release.

## Dependency order and critical path

Identity/tenant boundaries → ready media + rights → schedule validation → immutable publication/EPG → playout/destination approval → ad insertion + inventory rights → measured impressions → reconciliation → statements → paid settlement.

Billing plans → durable usage → versioned price calculation → invoice/payment reconciliation is a parallel SaaS revenue path from R2. Finance and ad reporting share references and audit conventions, but keep their ledgers and obligations separate.

Critical external risks: media/SSAI access, destination acceptance, demand agreements, rights clearance and payment availability. Product must show provider readiness and blocked dependencies explicitly. Prototype simulators keep discovery moving; they cannot satisfy production gates.

## First engineering sprint after planning

Create a single frontend and Java service; add PostgreSQL migrations, two fictional organizations, OIDC development integration and server-enforced memberships. Deliver asset metadata + rights + channel draft persisted through the API. Cover cross-tenant rejection and rights expiry with integration tests. Publish setup instructions and capture a working demo. Do not begin all modules or add media-scale infrastructure in the first sprint.

## Explicit deferrals

Defer a proprietary SSAI engine, RTB exchange, TV/mobile app suite, recommendation engine, microservices, Kafka, Kubernetes, marketplace payouts and autonomous AI publishing. Revisit each only when a paying requirement or measured scaling constraint justifies it.

30. Acceptance criteria for the complete prototype

The prototype is considered ready for a leadership demonstration when the following conditions are met.

Authentication

• Sign-in and sign-out work.
• Invalid credentials produce a useful error.
• Demo roles can be selected through a documented development workflow.
• Protected routes are handled correctly.

Content management

• Assets can be created and edited.
• Search and filters work.
• Asset states are visible.
• Asset references remain consistent across the application.
• Destructive operations respect dependencies.

Channel management

• Channels can be created and edited.
• Status transitions are validated.
• Channel details link to the corresponding schedules and rights.
• Channel previews display relevant metadata.

Scheduling

• Schedule entries can be created and edited.
• Invalid overlaps and durations are detected.
• Rights conflicts produce understandable warnings or errors.
• Schedule publication creates a version.
• EPG data corresponds to the published schedule.

Geo-blocking

• Allowed and blocked territories are configurable.
• Rights windows are respected.
• The effective policy is explained in the result.
• The prototype clearly labels its location checks as simulated.
• Production enforcement requirements are documented.

Advertising

• Campaigns and ad breaks can be configured.
• Simulated events are clearly labelled.
• Analytics use defined formulas and data sources.

Distribution and monitoring

• Destinations can be configured.
• Publication simulations create records.
• Alerts can be acknowledged and resolved.
• Simulated and real events are distinguishable.

Quality

• Changes persist according to the selected storage strategy.
• Navigation works.
• No major control is inert.
• Empty, loading and error states are handled.
• The application has a consistent design system.
• Automated tests cover the critical business logic.
• Setup and demonstration instructions are complete.

31. Risks and decisions to resolve

Codex must maintain an explicit assumptions register.

Product risks

• The target customer is not yet confirmed.
• The expected commercial model is not yet confirmed.
• The actual scope of the director's request may be larger or smaller than this proposal.
• Some features may be unnecessary for the first release.

Technical risks

• Building a real playout engine is significantly more complex than building the dashboard.
• Media transcoding, packaging and delivery may require third-party services.
• Scheduling and rights evaluation contain time-zone and policy edge cases.
• Real advertising integration depends on the selected provider and delivery model.
• Distribution integrations may require commercial access or partner approval.
• Browser-only storage cannot provide production-grade multi-user security.

Decisions required

1. Is the intended deliverable a UI prototype, a backend-backed proof of concept, or a production product?
2. Which Java backend services and infrastructure already exist at Enveu and can be reused?
3. Does the company want to build playout internally or integrate a provider?
4. Who is the primary customer?
5. Which distribution destinations are essential?
6. What is the expected MVP timeline?
7. Is real video playback required in the first demonstration?
8. Is real geo-enforcement required or is an explicitly labelled simulation acceptable?
9. Which ad insertion model and providers are being considered?
10. Which features are commercially mandatory for the first release?

Do not block all progress while waiting for every answer. Make reversible assumptions, document them and prioritize the decisions that materially affect architecture.
